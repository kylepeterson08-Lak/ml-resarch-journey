# AP Gov Study Guide

**AP U.S. Government and Politics · 2026–27 course framework**  
117 flashcards across all five units, including the 14 required Supreme Court cases and the 13 required foundational documents.

## How to use this guide

- **Studying alone:** Read the term, say the answer out loud, then check it. Mark anything you missed and come back to it in a few days.
- **Parent check-in:** Each card ends with a *Parent check-in* box. Read the question aloud, compare the answer to the card, then ask the follow-up. The follow-up tests the “why,” which is what the free-response section rewards.
- **Quick review:** The tables of required cases and documents and the rapid-fire self-test at the end are built for the week before the exam.

## The four free-response questions

| # | Question | Time | What it asks | Practice with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Concept Application | ~20 min | A short political scenario. Explain how it connects to a principle, institution, or process, and how another branch or actor could respond. | Checks and balances, federalism, Congress and the president |
| 2 | Quantitative Analysis | ~20 min | A chart, table, or map. Describe the data, find a trend, draw a conclusion, and connect it to a political concept. | Polling, turnout, budget |
| 3 | SCOTUS Comparison | ~20 min | A case you haven’t studied is described. Compare it to a required case and explain how the earlier ruling applies. | The 14 required cases: facts, clause, holding |
| 4 | Argument Essay | ~40 min | Take a defensible position, support it with at least two pieces of evidence (one from the foundational documents listed in the prompt), and respond to an opposing view. | The 13 foundational documents |

## Unit 1: Foundations of American Democracy

*15–22% of the multiple-choice section · 34 cards*

### Ideals of democracy

#### Limited government

**Answer.** The idea that government’s power is restricted by law, usually a written constitution, so it can’t take away people’s basic rights.

- **Why it matters:** It’s the thread through the whole Constitution. Separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism, and the Bill of Rights all exist to keep power limited.
- **Real-life hook:** It’s why a principal or police officer can’t search anything they want, any time, for any reason. Power comes with rules.
- **On the exam:** Shows up constantly in the Argument Essay (FRQ 4). Pair it with the Declaration, the Constitution, or Federalist 51 as evidence.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Limited government,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name two features of the Constitution that limit government.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Any two: separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism, enumerated powers, the Bill of Rights, regular elections.

#### Natural rights

**Answer.** Rights people are born with that no government grants and none can legitimately take away. John Locke listed them as life, liberty, and property.

- **Why it matters:** Jefferson put this idea at the center of the Declaration of Independence (“life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness”). It justified breaking away from Britain.
- **On the exam:** Expect a multiple-choice passage from the Declaration asking which Enlightenment idea it reflects.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Natural rights,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** According to the Declaration, what may people do if a government violates these rights?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** “Alter or abolish” it and set up a new government. That’s the social contract at work.

#### Social contract

**Answer.** The deal in which people agree to give up some freedom and obey a government in exchange for protection of their rights. If the government breaks the deal, the people may replace it.

- **Why it matters:** It comes from Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau, and it explains why American government rests on the consent of the governed.
- **Real-life hook:** Think of school rules: you accept a bell schedule and a code of conduct in exchange for a safe school that works.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Social contract,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How does the social contract connect to popular sovereignty?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Both say government’s authority comes from the people’s consent. People create the government, so power flows from them.

#### Popular sovereignty

**Answer.** The principle that government’s authority comes from the people.

- **Why it matters:** The Constitution opens with “We the People,” not “We the States.” Elections and the amendment process put this idea into practice.
- **On the exam:** A classic multiple-choice pairing: the Preamble or the Gettysburg Address → popular sovereignty.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Popular sovereignty,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give one place popular sovereignty appears in the Constitution or in practice.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Preamble (“We the People”), elections, ratification by state conventions, the amendment process, or state ballot initiatives.

#### Republicanism

**Answer.** A system in which citizens elect representatives to make laws for them, instead of voting on every law directly.

- **Why it matters:** The Framers chose a republic over direct democracy because they feared fast-moving majorities. Madison defends it in Federalist 10.
- **Real-life hook:** You’ll vote for a member of Congress. You won’t vote on each bill they pass.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Republicanism,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why did Madison think a large republic was better than a direct democracy?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Representatives filter public passions, and a large republic contains so many factions that no single one can easily dominate.

#### Declaration of Independence (1776)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** Thomas Jefferson’s statement that people have natural rights, that government gets its power from the consent of the governed, and that people may abolish a government that abuses those rights. It lists grievances against King George III.

- **Why it matters:** It applies Locke’s ideas of natural rights and the social contract. It explains why the colonies broke away, though it doesn’t set up a government.
- **On the exam:** Strong Argument Essay evidence for limited government, natural rights, or popular sovereignty.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Declaration of Independence (1776)?
>
> **Follow-up:** The Declaration created no government. Why is it still a foundational document?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It states the core principles (natural rights, consent of the governed, the social contract) that the Constitution was later built to protect.

#### Gettysburg Address (Lincoln, 1863)

*Foundational document · New for 2026–27*

**Answer.** Lincoln’s short speech at a Civil War battlefield. It ties the war to the Declaration’s promise that “all men are created equal” and calls for “a new birth of freedom” so that “government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth.”

- **Why it matters:** It reframes the Union as a commitment to equality and popular sovereignty. It’s about 270 words, so read the whole thing.
- **On the exam:** New to the required list for 2026–27. Strong evidence for popular sovereignty, equality, or national unity.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Gettysburg Address (Lincoln, 1863)?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which earlier foundational document does the Gettysburg Address echo, and how?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Declaration of Independence. “Four score and seven years ago” points back to 1776, and Lincoln repeats its claim that all men are created equal.

### Models of democracy

#### Participatory democracy

**Answer.** A model that emphasizes broad participation in politics and civil society by as many citizens as possible.

- **Why it matters:** One of three models of representative democracy on the exam. Examples: state ballot initiatives, referendums, recall elections, town halls.
- **On the exam:** The exam loves “Which model does this scenario show?” Broad citizen involvement = participatory.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Participatory democracy,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give a real example of participatory democracy in action.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** A state ballot initiative or referendum, a recall election, a town hall meeting, or a grassroots campaign.

#### Pluralist democracy

**Answer.** A model in which organized groups compete to influence policy, and no single group dominates.

- **Why it matters:** Interest groups like AARP, the NRA, and the Sierra Club fit here. Federalist 10’s argument about many competing factions supports this view.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Pluralist democracy,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why might a pluralist argue that interest groups are good for democracy?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Groups give ordinary people a louder collective voice, and competition among them keeps any one group from controlling policy.

#### Elite democracy

**Answer.** A model in which a small group of wealthy, educated, or well-connected people has outsized influence over political decisions.

- **Why it matters:** The Framers built in elite features: the Electoral College and (until the 17th Amendment in 1913) senators chosen by state legislatures. Brutus 1 warned a distant national government would serve elites.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Elite democracy,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name one feature of the original Constitution that reflects elite democracy.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Electoral College, or senators chosen by state legislatures before the 17th Amendment.

### Articles & Constitution

#### Articles of Confederation (1781)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** The first U.S. constitution, in effect until 1789. It created a weak national government: no power to tax, no national army, no executive or national courts, no power to regulate trade between states, and amendments required all 13 states.

- **Why it matters:** Its failures, made obvious by Shays’ Rebellion, led directly to the Constitutional Convention. Each weakness maps to a fix in the Constitution.
- **On the exam:** A common question: how did the Constitution fix a specific weakness of the Articles?

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Articles of Confederation (1781)?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name a weakness of the Articles and how the Constitution fixed it.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** No power to tax → Congress can tax (Art. I, Sec. 8). No executive → a president (Art. II). No national courts → Supreme Court (Art. III). Unanimity to amend → Article V’s three-fourths rule.

#### Shays’ Rebellion (1786–87)

**Answer.** An armed uprising of indebted Massachusetts farmers. The national government under the Articles had no army or money to stop it.

- **Why it matters:** It exposed how weak the Articles of Confederation were and pushed leaders to call the Constitutional Convention in 1787.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Shays’ Rebellion (1786–87),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What weaknesses of the Articles did Shays’ Rebellion reveal?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** No power to tax, no national army or executive to enforce laws, and no way to respond quickly to a crisis.

#### Great (Connecticut) Compromise

**Answer.** The agreement that created a two-house Congress: a House of Representatives based on state population and a Senate with two senators per state.

- **Why it matters:** It settled the fight between large states (Virginia Plan) and small states (New Jersey Plan). It’s why Wyoming and California each have two senators.
- **On the exam:** Be ready to explain which plan each house came from.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Great (Connecticut) Compromise,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which house reflects the Virginia Plan, and which reflects the New Jersey Plan?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** House = Virginia Plan (population). Senate = New Jersey Plan (equal representation).

#### Three-Fifths Compromise

**Answer.** The agreement to count enslaved people as three-fifths of a person when apportioning House seats and direct taxes.

- **Why it matters:** It boosted Southern states’ power in the House and the Electoral College. The 13th and 14th Amendments ended it after the Civil War.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Three-Fifths Compromise,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How did this compromise affect presidential elections, not just the House?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Electoral votes equal a state’s House seats plus Senate seats, so extra House seats gave Southern states extra electoral votes.

#### U.S. Constitution (1787)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** The framework of the national government. It creates three branches, divides power between the nation and the states, and sets out how it can be amended. The Bill of Rights was added in 1791.

- **Why it matters:** It is the source for nearly every concept on the exam: separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism, enumerated powers, and individual rights.
- **On the exam:** Know the articles: I Congress, II President, III Courts, IV relations among states, V amendments, VI supremacy, VII ratification.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of U.S. Constitution (1787)?
>
> **Follow-up:** What does Article VI establish, and why does it matter?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Supremacy Clause. Valid federal law beats conflicting state law, which decided cases like McCulloch v. Maryland.

#### Amendment process (Article V)

**Answer.** Amendments are proposed by two-thirds of both houses of Congress (or a convention called by two-thirds of the states) and ratified by three-fourths of the states.

- **Why it matters:** The supermajority requirements make the Constitution hard to change, which is why there are only 27 amendments.
- **On the exam:** Know the numbers: two-thirds to propose, three-fourths to ratify.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Amendment process (Article V),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why did the Framers make amending the Constitution so difficult?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** To protect it from temporary majorities and to require broad national agreement before changing the basic rules.

#### Separation of powers

**Answer.** Dividing government into three branches: legislative (makes laws), executive (enforces laws), and judicial (interprets laws).

- **Why it matters:** Madison argued in Federalist 51 that splitting power prevents tyranny. Each branch has its own article: I, II, and III.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Separation of powers,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which articles of the Constitution set up each branch?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Article I: Congress. Article II: the President. Article III: the courts.

#### Checks and balances

**Answer.** Each branch has tools to limit the others, such as the presidential veto, the congressional override, Senate confirmation, impeachment, and judicial review.

- **Why it matters:** The goal is to make “ambition counteract ambition” (Federalist 51), so no branch can dominate.
- **On the exam:** Concept Application (FRQ 1) often gives a scenario and asks how another branch could respond. Have two or three checks ready for each branch.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Checks and balances,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give one check Congress has on the president and one check the courts have on Congress.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Congress: override a veto (two-thirds of both houses), reject appointments, impeach, control funding. Courts: strike down a law as unconstitutional.

#### Federalist No. 51 (Madison, 1788)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** Madison argues that separation of powers and checks and balances will protect liberty: “Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.” Federalism adds a “double security” by dividing power between nation and states.

- **Why it matters:** Its famous line, “If men were angels, no government would be necessary,” explains why the Constitution assumes people will seek power and builds in ways to stop them.
- **On the exam:** Go-to evidence for any argument about checks and balances, separation of powers, or limiting government.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Federalist No. 51 (Madison, 1788)?
>
> **Follow-up:** What does “ambition must be made to counteract ambition” mean in practice?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Each branch’s officials will defend their own branch’s power, which keeps any other branch from taking too much.

#### Federalist No. 10 (Madison, 1787)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** Madison argues that factions (groups pursuing their own interests) are unavoidable. The cure is to control their effects with a large republic, where so many factions compete that no single one can take over.

- **Why it matters:** The key defense of a large republic and a foundation for the pluralist view of democracy. It answers Anti-Federalist fears like those in Brutus 1.
- **On the exam:** Federalist 10 vs. Brutus 1 is one of the most tested comparisons on the exam.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Federalist No. 10 (Madison, 1787)?
>
> **Follow-up:** Madison says you can’t remove the causes of faction. Why not, and what does he do instead?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Removing the causes would mean destroying liberty or making everyone think alike. Instead, a large republic controls the effects.

#### Brutus No. 1 (Anti-Federalist, 1787)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** Argues that a large republic can’t work. The Necessary and Proper and Supremacy Clauses would give the national government nearly unlimited power, and a distant government would serve elites instead of ordinary people.

- **Why it matters:** The leading Anti-Federalist essay. Its fears helped win the Bill of Rights, and its debate with Federalist 10 still frames arguments about national vs. state power.
- **On the exam:** If an essay prompt involves states’ rights or limits on national power, Brutus 1 is strong evidence.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Brutus No. 1 (Anti-Federalist, 1787)?
>
> **Follow-up:** What would Brutus think of the Supreme Court’s decision in McCulloch v. Maryland?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** He’d see it as proof of his fear: the Court used the Necessary and Proper and Supremacy Clauses to expand national power over the states.

### Federalism

#### Federalist No. 39 (Madison, 1788)

*Foundational document · New for 2026–27*

**Answer.** Madison defines a republic as a government that gets its power from the people and is run by officials who serve limited terms or during good behavior. He argues the Constitution is “neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both.”

- **Why it matters:** It answers critics who said the Constitution would wipe out the states. Madison shows that some parts draw power from the people (the House), some from the states as equals (the Senate), and some from both (amendments).
- **On the exam:** New to the required list for 2026–27. Use it as evidence on federalism or popular sovereignty questions.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Federalist No. 39 (Madison, 1788)?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give one example of how the Constitution is “partly national, partly federal.”
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The House draws power directly from the people (national); the Senate gives states equal representation (federal); amendments need both Congress and the states.

#### Federalism

**Answer.** Power divided between a national government and state governments, each with its own authority over the same people.

- **Why it matters:** Madison called it a “double security” for rights in Federalist 51. The balance between national and state power has shifted constantly since 1787.
- **Real-life hook:** Your state sets the rules for your driver’s license. The national government prints your money.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Federalism,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give one power that belongs to the national government and one reserved to the states.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** National: coining money, declaring war, regulating interstate commerce, making treaties. States: running elections, issuing licenses, setting up schools and local governments.

#### Enumerated (expressed) powers

**Answer.** Powers specifically listed for Congress in the Constitution, mostly in Article I, Section 8: taxing, borrowing, coining money, regulating interstate commerce, declaring war, and more.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Enumerated (expressed) powers,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Is “creating a national bank” one of these powers? How did Congress justify it?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** No. Congress justified it as an implied power under the Necessary and Proper Clause, upheld in McCulloch v. Maryland.

#### Necessary and Proper (Elastic) Clause

**Answer.** The last clause of Article I, Section 8. It lets Congress make all laws “necessary and proper” for carrying out its listed powers, which creates implied powers.

- **Why it matters:** It stretches national power, which is why it’s called the elastic clause. McCulloch v. Maryland upheld it. Brutus 1 feared it would give Congress nearly unlimited power.
- **On the exam:** This clause links McCulloch (a required case) and Brutus 1 (a required document). That’s a ready-made essay connection.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Necessary and Proper (Elastic) Clause,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why did Anti-Federalists like Brutus fear this clause?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** They thought its vague wording would let the national government expand without limit and swallow up state power.

#### Reserved powers (10th Amendment)

**Answer.** Powers not given to the national government, nor denied to the states, are reserved to the states or to the people.

- **Why it matters:** It’s the constitutional basis for state control over schools, policing, marriage law, and elections. States cite it whenever they push back on federal power.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Reserved powers (10th Amendment),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which required case is a win for state power under this idea?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** United States v. Lopez (1995). The Court said Congress overstepped the Commerce Clause, leaving guns near schools to state law.

#### Commerce Clause

**Answer.** Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to regulate commerce “among the several States.” Courts have read it broadly to expand federal power.

- **Why it matters:** Congress used it to pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (banning discrimination at restaurants and hotels) and much environmental law. Lopez (1995) marked a limit.
- **On the exam:** Questions often pair McCulloch (national power grows) with Lopez (national power has limits).

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Commerce Clause,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How is Lopez different from most earlier cases about this clause?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** For decades the Court let Congress stretch it. In Lopez it said carrying a gun near a school isn’t economic activity, so Congress went too far.

#### Supremacy Clause

**Answer.** Article VI says the Constitution, federal laws, and treaties are “the supreme Law of the Land.” When a valid federal law conflicts with a state law, federal law wins.

- **Why it matters:** It’s why Maryland lost in McCulloch v. Maryland: a state can’t tax or obstruct a legitimate federal institution.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Supremacy Clause,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** In McCulloch, which two constitutional clauses did the Court rely on?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Necessary and Proper Clause (Congress could create the bank) and the Supremacy Clause (Maryland couldn’t tax it).

#### Categorical vs. block grants

**Answer.** Categorical grants give states federal money for a specific purpose with strict conditions. Block grants give money for a broad purpose with more state flexibility.

- **Why it matters:** This is fiscal federalism: Washington uses money to shape state policy. States usually prefer block grants because they keep more control.
- **Real-life hook:** School lunch programs and special education services run partly on federal grant money with federal rules attached.
- **On the exam:** A favorite Concept Application scenario. Know which type each side of a debate prefers and why.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Categorical vs. block grants.
>
> **Follow-up:** Why would a governor prefer a block grant over a categorical grant?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Block grants let the state decide how to spend the money to fit local needs, with fewer federal strings.

#### Mandates and unfunded mandates

**Answer.** Federal requirements that states must follow. When Congress doesn’t provide money to pay for them, they’re called unfunded mandates.

- **Why it matters:** The Americans with Disabilities Act (1990) is the textbook example: it requires state and local governments to make buildings, programs, and services accessible, largely at their own expense.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Mandates and unfunded mandates,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why do states often object to unfunded mandates?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** States must pay to carry out federal policy without federal money, which strains budgets and limits state control.

#### Conditions of aid

**Answer.** Rules a state must accept to receive federal money. In 1984 Congress threatened to withhold some highway funds from any state with a drinking age below 21. Every state complied.

- **Why it matters:** The Supreme Court upheld this in South Dakota v. Dole (1987). It shows how the national government can shape policy in areas it doesn’t directly control.
- **Real-life hook:** This is why the drinking age is 21 in all 50 states, even though no federal law sets it.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Conditions of aid,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why didn’t Congress just pass a national drinking-age law?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Setting a drinking age is generally a state power, so Congress used its spending power and attached conditions to highway money.

#### Dual vs. cooperative federalism

**Answer.** Dual (“layer cake”) federalism keeps national and state powers in separate spheres. Cooperative (“marble cake”) federalism has both levels working together on shared policies, often through grants.

- **Why it matters:** The U.S. moved from mostly dual federalism toward cooperative federalism during the New Deal of the 1930s.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Dual vs. cooperative federalism.
>
> **Follow-up:** Give an example of cooperative federalism.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Medicaid (paid for jointly by federal and state governments), highway funding, or education grants.

#### McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Maryland tried to tax the Second Bank of the United States. The Court ruled unanimously that Congress had implied power to create the bank (Necessary and Proper Clause) and that a state can’t tax a federal institution (Supremacy Clause).

- **Why it matters:** It expanded national power over the states. Chief Justice Marshall wrote that “the power to tax involves the power to destroy.”
- **On the exam:** Clauses: Necessary and Proper + Supremacy. Pairs with Brutus 1, Federalist 10, and federalism questions.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which level of government won, and why does that still matter?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The national government. It set the precedent that Congress has implied powers and that valid federal law overrides state law.

#### United States v. Lopez (1995)

*Required case*

**Answer.** A high school senior in San Antonio was charged under the federal Gun-Free School Zones Act of 1990 for bringing a gun to school. The Court ruled 5–4 that Congress had exceeded its Commerce Clause power, because carrying a gun near a school isn’t economic activity.

- **Why it matters:** The first time in about 60 years the Court limited Congress’s Commerce Clause power. A win for states’ reserved powers.
- **Real-life hook:** The student at the center of this case was a 12th grader.
- **On the exam:** Contrast with McCulloch: McCulloch expanded national power; Lopez limited it.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in United States v. Lopez (1995), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why couldn’t Congress use the Commerce Clause here?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Bringing a gun to school isn’t commercial activity that substantially affects interstate commerce, so it falls under state police powers.

## Unit 2: Interactions Among Branches of Government

*25–36% of the multiple-choice section · 35 cards*

### Congress

#### House vs. Senate

**Answer.** House: 435 members, 2-year terms, seats based on population, minimum age 25. Senate: 100 members (two per state), 6-year terms with one-third elected every two years, minimum age 30.

- **Why it matters:** The House was designed to respond quickly to public opinion; the Senate was designed to be slower and more deliberate.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: House vs. Senate.
>
> **Follow-up:** Name one power only the House has and one only the Senate has.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** House: starts revenue (tax) bills, impeaches. Senate: confirms appointments, ratifies treaties (two-thirds vote), holds impeachment trials.

#### Filibuster and cloture

**Answer.** A filibuster is a Senate tactic of extended debate to delay or block a vote. Cloture ends debate and requires 60 votes.

- **Why it matters:** Because of the filibuster, most major bills effectively need 60 Senate votes, not a simple majority of 51. The House has no filibuster because its Rules Committee limits debate.
- **Real-life hook:** When the news says a bill “doesn’t have 60 votes,” this is why.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Filibuster and cloture,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why doesn’t the House have a filibuster?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The House is much larger, so its Rules Committee sets strict time limits on debate for each bill.

#### House Rules Committee

**Answer.** The House committee that decides when a bill reaches the floor, how long it can be debated, and whether amendments are allowed.

- **Why it matters:** It’s the House’s traffic cop. The majority party controls it, which gives the Speaker major power over what gets a vote.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “House Rules Committee,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How does this committee give the majority party power?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It can keep bills it dislikes off the floor and block amendments that would change bills it supports.

#### Congressional committees

**Answer.** Small groups of members that review bills in specific policy areas (standing committees), hold hearings, and decide which bills move forward. Conference committees work out differences between House and Senate versions.

- **Why it matters:** Most bills die in committee. Committees let members specialize and are where Congress does most of its oversight of the executive branch.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Congressional committees,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why do most bills die in committee?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Committees screen thousands of bills and advance only the ones the chair and party leaders support or have time for.

#### Mandatory vs. discretionary spending

**Answer.** Mandatory spending is required by existing law, mostly entitlements like Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid. Discretionary spending is set each year through appropriations bills, including defense and education.

- **Why it matters:** Mandatory spending plus interest on the debt is roughly 70% of the federal budget and growing, which shrinks what Congress decides each year. That squeeze drives budget fights.
- **Real-life hook:** Look at your first paycheck: the FICA line (7.65% = 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare) funds the biggest mandatory programs.
- **On the exam:** The Quantitative Analysis FRQ often uses budget charts. Know which programs fall in which category.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Mandatory vs. discretionary spending.
>
> **Follow-up:** Why is it hard to cut the deficit by trimming discretionary spending alone?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Discretionary spending is the smaller share. The biggest, fastest-growing costs are entitlements and interest, which change only if Congress changes the underlying laws.

#### Reapportionment vs. redistricting

**Answer.** Reapportionment reassigns the 435 House seats among the states after each census (every 10 years). Redistricting is when states redraw district lines to match the new seat counts and population shifts.

- **Why it matters:** Whoever draws the lines can influence who wins. That leads directly to gerrymandering and to Baker v. Carr and Shaw v. Reno.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Reapportionment vs. redistricting.
>
> **Follow-up:** Who usually draws congressional district lines?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** State legislatures, though some states use independent or bipartisan commissions.

#### Gerrymandering

**Answer.** Drawing district lines to give one party or group an advantage. Two techniques: packing (concentrating opponents in a few districts) and cracking (spreading them thin across many).

- **Why it matters:** Racial gerrymanders can violate Equal Protection (Shaw v. Reno). In Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), the Court said federal courts won’t decide partisan gerrymandering claims.
- **On the exam:** Link it to Baker v. Carr and Shaw v. Reno on the SCOTUS Comparison FRQ.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Gerrymandering,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What’s the difference between packing and cracking?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Packing crowds the other side’s voters into a few districts they win by huge margins; cracking splits them across many districts so they can’t win any.

#### Baker v. Carr (1962)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Tennessee hadn’t redrawn its legislative districts since 1901, so rural votes counted far more than city votes. The Court ruled that redistricting challenges are justiciable: federal courts can hear them under the Equal Protection Clause.

- **Why it matters:** It opened the door to the “one person, one vote” rulings that followed, such as Reynolds v. Sims (1964).
- **On the exam:** Pair with Shaw v. Reno. Both involve redistricting and the 14th Amendment.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Baker v. Carr (1962), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** What does “justiciable” mean here, and why did it matter?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It means a court can decide the issue. Before Baker, courts treated districting as a political question; after Baker, voters could sue over unequal districts.

#### Shaw v. Reno (1993)

*Required case*

**Answer.** North Carolina drew a long, snaking majority-Black congressional district. The Court ruled that districts drawn mainly on the basis of race must pass strict scrutiny under the Equal Protection Clause.

- **Why it matters:** It limits racial gerrymandering, even when the goal is to increase minority representation.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Shaw v. Reno (1993), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** What’s the constitutional basis of this decision?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The 14th Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause.

#### Trustee, delegate, and politico

**Answer.** Three models of how lawmakers vote. Trustee: uses their own judgment. Delegate: votes the way constituents want. Politico: balances the two depending on the issue.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Trustee, delegate, and politico,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** A senator votes against a bill most of her state supports because she thinks it’s bad policy. Which model is that?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Trustee.

#### Divided government and gridlock

**Answer.** Divided government means one party holds the presidency and the other controls at least one house of Congress. Gridlock is the resulting difficulty in passing laws.

- **Why it matters:** It affects confirmations, budgets, and how often vetoes happen. A common Concept Application scenario.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Divided government and gridlock,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How might divided government affect judicial confirmations?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** A Senate controlled by the other party can delay or reject the president’s nominees.

### Presidency

#### President’s formal powers

**Answer.** Powers listed in Article II: commander in chief, veto bills, make treaties (with Senate approval), appoint officials and judges (with Senate approval), grant pardons, and receive ambassadors.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Name the president’s formal powers from Article II.
>
> **Follow-up:** Which formal powers require Senate approval?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Treaties (two-thirds vote) and major appointments such as cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and federal judges (majority vote).

#### President’s informal powers

**Answer.** Powers not listed in the Constitution but used by presidents: executive orders, executive agreements, signing statements, and the bully pulpit.

- **Why it matters:** Presidents have expanded these powers over time, especially when Congress is gridlocked.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What are the president’s informal powers? Give examples.
>
> **Follow-up:** Why have informal powers grown?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Congress is often slow or divided, so presidents act on their own, and modern media amplifies the president’s voice.

#### Executive order

**Answer.** A directive from the president to the executive branch that carries the force of law, based on authority from the Constitution or from a law Congress passed.

- **Why it matters:** It’s fast and needs no one’s approval, but it’s fragile. A new president can reverse it, Congress can override it by law, and courts can strike it down.
- **On the exam:** A classic FRQ 1 task: “Explain how Congress or the courts could check this action.”

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Executive order,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name two ways this kind of action can be checked.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Courts can rule it unconstitutional or beyond the president’s authority; Congress can pass a law overriding it or cut its funding; a later president can revoke it.

#### Executive agreement

**Answer.** An agreement between the president and a foreign leader that doesn’t require Senate ratification.

- **Why it matters:** Presidents use them to avoid the two-thirds Senate vote that treaties need. They are less permanent than treaties.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Executive agreement,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why would a president use this instead of a treaty?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** To avoid needing two-thirds of the Senate, which is hard to get, especially in divided government.

#### Veto and pocket veto

**Answer.** A veto is the president’s rejection of a bill, which Congress can override with a two-thirds vote in both houses. A pocket veto happens when the president doesn’t sign a bill within 10 days (Sundays excepted) and Congress has adjourned, so the bill dies with no chance of override.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Veto and pocket veto,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why is a pocket veto stronger than a regular veto?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Congress has adjourned, so it can’t vote to override.

#### War Powers Resolution (1973)

**Answer.** A law passed after the Vietnam War. The president must notify Congress within 48 hours of sending troops into combat and must withdraw them within 60 days (plus a 30-day withdrawal period) unless Congress approves.

- **Why it matters:** Congress declares war, but the president is commander in chief. This law was Congress’s attempt to rebalance that tension. Presidents of both parties have questioned whether it’s constitutional.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “War Powers Resolution (1973),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which two constitutional roles does this law try to balance?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Congress’s power to declare war and the president’s role as commander in chief.

#### Bully pulpit

**Answer.** The president’s ability to use the visibility of the office to persuade the public and pressure Congress.

- **Why it matters:** Theodore Roosevelt coined the phrase. Today it includes televised addresses, the State of the Union, and social media.
- **Real-life hook:** Every presidential post that trends online is this power at work.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Bully pulpit,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How does it help a president get legislation passed?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** By rallying public support, which pressures members of Congress who worry about reelection.

#### Impeachment and removal

**Answer.** The House impeaches (formally charges) by a simple majority. The Senate holds a trial and removes the official only with a two-thirds vote.

- **Why it matters:** It’s Congress’s ultimate check on the executive and judicial branches. The Senate has never removed a president.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Impeachment and removal,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which house acts like a prosecutor, and which acts like a jury?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The House brings charges, like a prosecutor; the Senate holds the trial, like a jury.

#### 22nd Amendment

**Answer.** Ratified in 1951, it limits a president to two elected terms.

- **Why it matters:** Passed after Franklin Roosevelt won four elections. It reflects a long-running fear, shared by the Anti-Federalists, of concentrated executive power.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “22nd Amendment,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which foundational document makes the case for a strong executive?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Federalist No. 70, by Alexander Hamilton.

#### Federalist No. 70 (Hamilton, 1788)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** Hamilton argues for a single, energetic executive. “Energy in the executive” means acting quickly and decisively, and one person (not a council) is easier to hold accountable.

- **Why it matters:** It defends the presidency against Anti-Federalist fears of a king. Use it to explain why presidential power has grown.
- **On the exam:** Strong Argument Essay evidence on presidential power or accountability.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Federalist No. 70 (Hamilton, 1788)?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why does Hamilton prefer one president over an executive council?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** One executive can act with speed and decisiveness, and it’s clear who gets the blame or credit, which makes the office accountable.

#### Emancipation Proclamation (Lincoln, 1863)

*Foundational document · New for 2026–27*

**Answer.** Lincoln’s order, effective January 1, 1863, declaring enslaved people in the states in rebellion “forever free.” Issued as a war measure under his power as commander in chief, it didn’t apply to the border states loyal to the Union.

- **Why it matters:** It turned the Civil War into a war against slavery and opened the Union army to Black soldiers. Because it was a wartime executive action, the 13th Amendment (1865) was needed to end slavery permanently everywhere.
- **On the exam:** New to the required list for 2026–27. Use it for civil rights and for the reach and limits of presidential power.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Emancipation Proclamation (Lincoln, 1863)?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why was the 13th Amendment still needed after the Proclamation?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Proclamation was a wartime executive action that covered only rebelling states and could have been challenged after the war. An amendment made abolition permanent and nationwide.

### Courts

#### Judicial review

**Answer.** The power of courts to strike down laws or government actions that violate the Constitution.

- **Why it matters:** It isn’t written in the Constitution. Hamilton defended it in Federalist 78, and the Court claimed it in Marbury v. Madison (1803).

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Judicial review,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Where does this power come from, if the Constitution doesn’t mention it?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Marbury v. Madison (1803), building on Hamilton’s argument in Federalist 78.

#### Marbury v. Madison (1803)

*Required case*

**Answer.** William Marbury sued for a judicial appointment that was never delivered. The Court ruled that the part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 he relied on was unconstitutional, establishing judicial review.

- **Why it matters:** Chief Justice Marshall gave up a small win (Marbury’s job) to claim a huge power for the Court: deciding what the Constitution means.
- **On the exam:** Pairs with Federalist 78. Any question about the courts’ power traces back here.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Marbury v. Madison (1803), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** How did Marshall turn a political loss into a long-term win for the Court?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** He ruled the Court couldn’t give Marbury his commission because the law granting that power was unconstitutional, which established the Court’s power to strike down laws.

#### Federalist No. 78 (Hamilton, 1788)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** Hamilton argues the judiciary is the “least dangerous” branch because it has influence over neither “the sword” nor “the purse.” Judges should serve during good behavior (effectively for life) to stay independent, and courts should strike down laws that conflict with the Constitution.

- **Why it matters:** It’s the intellectual foundation for judicial review, later put into practice in Marbury v. Madison.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Federalist No. 78 (Hamilton, 1788)?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why does Hamilton think life tenure matters?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It frees judges from political pressure so they can protect the Constitution even when their rulings are unpopular.

#### Stare decisis (precedent)

**Answer.** Latin for “let the decision stand.” Courts generally follow earlier rulings on the same legal question.

- **Why it matters:** It keeps the law stable and predictable. The Court can overturn precedent, as Brown v. Board did to Plessy v. Ferguson and Dobbs did to Roe v. Wade.
- **On the exam:** The SCOTUS Comparison FRQ often asks how a precedent applies to a new case.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Stare decisis (precedent),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give an example of the Supreme Court overturning its own precedent.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Brown v. Board (1954) overturned Plessy v. Ferguson’s “separate but equal”; Dobbs (2022) overturned Roe v. Wade.

#### Judicial activism vs. restraint

**Answer.** Judicial activism: judges are willing to strike down laws or overturn precedent to fix what they see as constitutional problems. Judicial restraint: judges defer to the elected branches and to precedent unless a violation is clear.

- **Why it matters:** Neither label belongs to one party. Whether a ruling looks “activist” often depends on whether you like the result.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Judicial activism vs. restraint.
>
> **Follow-up:** Is overturning precedent always activism?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It’s usually labeled that way, but people disagree. Supporters of a reversal often call it correcting a mistake.

#### Writ of certiorari (rule of four)

**Answer.** The Supreme Court agrees to hear a case by granting a writ of certiorari. It takes four of the nine justices to grant one.

- **Why it matters:** The Court gets thousands of petitions each year and hears only about 60 cases. Most arrive on appeal from federal appeals courts or state supreme courts.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Writ of certiorari (rule of four),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Roughly how many cases does the Court hear each term?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** About 60, out of several thousand petitions.

#### Majority, concurring, and dissenting opinions

**Answer.** Majority opinion: the Court’s ruling and reasoning, which sets precedent. Concurring opinion: agrees with the result for different reasons. Dissenting opinion: disagrees with the result.

- **Why it matters:** Dissents sometimes become future majorities. Justice Harlan’s lone dissent in Plessy (1896) was vindicated by Brown in 1954.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Majority, concurring, and dissenting opinions,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why write a dissent if it doesn’t change the outcome?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** To lay out reasoning future courts might adopt, to signal disagreement, or to push Congress to act.

#### Checks on the courts

**Answer.** The president nominates and the Senate confirms judges. Congress can change the number of justices, limit which cases courts can hear, pass new laws, or propose amendments to reverse rulings. The executive branch decides how to enforce rulings.

- **Why it matters:** Federalist 78 calls courts the “least dangerous” branch because they control neither the sword nor the purse. Their power depends on legitimacy.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Checks on the courts,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How can Congress respond to a Supreme Court ruling it disagrees with?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Pass a new law (when the ruling interpreted a statute), propose a constitutional amendment, change the Court’s size or jurisdiction, or refuse to fund enforcement.

### Bureaucracy

#### Federal bureaucracy

**Answer.** The departments, agencies, and commissions that carry out federal laws. It includes 15 cabinet departments, independent regulatory agencies (like the FCC), and government corporations (like the U.S. Postal Service).

- **Why it matters:** Congress writes laws broadly, and the bureaucracy fills in the details by writing regulations. That gives unelected officials real power.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Federal bureaucracy,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why does Congress give agencies so much discretion?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Lawmakers lack the time and expertise for technical details, so they delegate rule-making to specialists.

#### Discretionary and rule-making authority

**Answer.** Agencies’ power to decide how to carry out laws and to write regulations that have the force of law. Example: the EPA setting pollution limits under the Clean Air Act.

- **Why it matters:** In Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024), the Supreme Court overturned the Chevron doctrine, so courts no longer defer to an agency’s reading of an unclear law. That shifted power from agencies to judges.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Discretionary and rule-making authority,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Who gained power when Chevron deference ended?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Federal courts, and arguably Congress, which now needs to write clearer laws. Agencies lost some freedom to interpret statutes.

#### Iron triangle

**Answer.** A stable, mutually beneficial relationship among a congressional committee, a federal agency, and an interest group in the same policy area.

- **Why it matters:** Example: the Armed Services committees, the Department of Defense, and defense contractors. Critics say these relationships serve insiders over the public. Issue networks are a looser, broader version.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Iron triangle,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What does each side of the triangle get?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The interest group gets favorable policy, the committee gets campaign support and expertise, and the agency gets funding and political backing.

#### Congressional oversight

**Answer.** Congress reviews how the executive branch carries out laws by holding hearings, investigating, controlling budgets (the “power of the purse”), and confirming appointees.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Congressional oversight,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What’s Congress’s most powerful oversight tool?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The power of the purse: it can increase, cut, or attach conditions to an agency’s funding.

#### Patronage vs. merit system

**Answer.** Patronage (the “spoils system”) gave government jobs to political supporters. The Pendleton Civil Service Act (1883) began replacing it with a merit system based on exams and qualifications.

- **Why it matters:** It passed after President Garfield was assassinated by a disappointed office seeker. It made the bureaucracy more professional and less political.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Patronage vs. merit system.
>
> **Follow-up:** What event pushed Congress to pass the Pendleton Act?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The 1881 assassination of President James Garfield by a man who had been denied a government job.

## Unit 3: Civil Liberties and Civil Rights

*13–18% of the multiple-choice section · 25 cards*

### Civil liberties

#### Civil liberties vs. civil rights

**Answer.** Civil liberties are protections from government interference, mostly in the Bill of Rights (speech, religion, fair trials). Civil rights are protections from unequal treatment, rooted in the 14th Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause.

- **Why it matters:** Mixing these up is one of the most common student mistakes. Liberties = freedom from government. Rights = equal treatment.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Civil liberties vs. civil rights.
>
> **Follow-up:** Is the right to a lawyer a civil liberty or a civil right?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** A civil liberty: a Sixth Amendment protection against the government.

#### Selective incorporation

**Answer.** The process of applying the Bill of Rights to the states, one right at a time, through the 14th Amendment’s Due Process Clause.

- **Why it matters:** The Bill of Rights originally limited only the national government. Incorporation means states can’t violate these rights either. Gideon and McDonald are required incorporation cases.
- **On the exam:** Know the clause: the Due Process Clause of the 14th Amendment.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Selective incorporation,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name two required cases that incorporated a right.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Gideon v. Wainwright (Sixth Amendment right to counsel) and McDonald v. Chicago (Second Amendment).

#### McDonald v. Chicago (2010)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Chicago banned handgun ownership. The Court ruled 5–4 that the Second Amendment right to keep and bear arms applies to state and local governments through the 14th Amendment’s Due Process Clause.

- **Why it matters:** It built on D.C. v. Heller (2008), which recognized an individual right to bear arms. A clear example of selective incorporation.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in McDonald v. Chicago (2010), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why did this case need to be decided after Heller?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Heller involved Washington, D.C., a federal district, so it didn’t settle whether the Second Amendment limited states and cities.

#### Right to privacy: Roe and Dobbs

*Context · not a required case*

**Answer.** The Constitution never says “privacy,” but Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) found an implied right. Roe v. Wade (1973) extended it to abortion. Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization (2022) overturned Roe and returned abortion regulation to the states.

- **Why it matters:** Roe is no longer a required case, but it still works as a non-required case on the SCOTUS Comparison FRQ and as a clear example of the Court overturning precedent.
- **On the exam:** Use it for stare decisis and federalism examples.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the right to privacy and what happened to Roe v. Wade.
>
> **Follow-up:** After Dobbs, who decides abortion policy?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Mostly the states, through state laws and ballot measures.

### First Amendment

#### Establishment Clause

**Answer.** The First Amendment bars government from establishing an official religion or favoring religion. Jefferson called it a “wall of separation between church and state.”

- **Why it matters:** Engel v. Vitale (1962) used it to ban official school prayer. The Court’s approach has shifted: in Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022) it protected a coach’s personal prayer on the field.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Establishment Clause,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How is Engel v. Vitale different from Kennedy v. Bremerton?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Engel struck down prayer written and sponsored by the state; Kennedy protected a public employee’s personal prayer, which the Court saw as private religious expression.

#### Engel v. Vitale (1962)

*Required case*

**Answer.** New York public schools began the day with a short, nondenominational prayer written by state officials. The Court ruled that school-sponsored prayer violates the Establishment Clause, even when it’s voluntary.

- **Why it matters:** Government can’t write or sponsor prayers in public schools. Students can still pray on their own.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Engel v. Vitale (1962), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** The prayer was voluntary and nondenominational. Why was it still unconstitutional?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The state wrote and sponsored it, which put the government’s support behind religion.

#### Free Exercise Clause

**Answer.** The First Amendment protects people’s right to practice their religion. Government can’t target religious practice, though some general laws still apply to everyone.

- **Why it matters:** Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972) is the required case: Amish families didn’t have to send their children to school past 8th grade.
- **On the exam:** When the two religion clauses collide, the exam may ask you to identify the tension.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Free Exercise Clause,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How can the two religion clauses conflict?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Accommodating religious practice (free exercise) can look like government favoring religion (establishment).

#### Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Amish parents refused to send their children to school past 8th grade, violating Wisconsin’s compulsory attendance law. The Court ruled that the Free Exercise Clause outweighed the state’s interest in two more years of schooling.

- **Why it matters:** It shows that religious freedom can override a general state law when the burden on religion is serious.
- **On the exam:** Engel and Yoder are both religion cases but different clauses: Engel = Establishment, Yoder = Free Exercise.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which religion clause does this case involve, and which does Engel involve?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Yoder: Free Exercise. Engel: Establishment.

#### Limits on free speech

**Answer.** Speech is protected but not absolute. Unprotected categories include true threats, defamation, obscenity, and incitement to “imminent lawless action” (Brandenburg v. Ohio, 1969).

- **Why it matters:** Schenck’s “clear and present danger” test (1919) was later replaced by Brandenburg’s stricter incitement test, which protects more speech.
- **Real-life hook:** You can criticize the government online. You can’t post a real threat against a specific person.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Limits on free speech,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which test replaced “clear and present danger,” and does it protect more or less speech?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Brandenburg’s “imminent lawless action” test. It protects more speech.

#### Schenck v. United States (1919)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Charles Schenck mailed leaflets urging men to resist the World War I draft. The Court unanimously upheld his conviction under the Espionage Act, ruling that speech creating a “clear and present danger” can be limited.

- **Why it matters:** Justice Holmes compared it to falsely shouting fire in a crowded theater. The test was later replaced by Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969).
- **On the exam:** The lesson: speech isn’t absolute, and context (like wartime) matters.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Schenck v. United States (1919), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Would the leaflets likely be protected today? Why?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Probably yes. Under Brandenburg, speech is protected unless it’s meant to, and likely to, produce imminent lawless action.

#### Symbolic speech

**Answer.** Actions that express a message, such as wearing armbands, burning a flag, or kneeling during the anthem, are protected as speech.

- **Why it matters:** Tinker v. Des Moines (1969) protected students’ armbands. Texas v. Johnson (1989) protected flag burning.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Symbolic speech,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** According to Tinker, when can schools limit student speech?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** When it would cause a “substantial disruption” of school activities or invade other students’ rights.

#### Tinker v. Des Moines (1969)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Students wore black armbands to school to protest the Vietnam War and were suspended. The Court ruled 7–2 that symbolic speech is protected and that students don’t “shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate.”

- **Why it matters:** Schools can limit student speech only if it causes substantial disruption. It’s the case behind most student-speech debates today.
- **Real-life hook:** Thinking of wearing a T-shirt with a political message to school? This is your case.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Tinker v. Des Moines (1969), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** What would a school need to show to legally ban a political T-shirt?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** That it would cause a substantial disruption of school activities or invade other students’ rights.

#### Prior restraint

**Answer.** Government action that blocks publication before it happens. Courts treat it as almost always unconstitutional.

- **Why it matters:** In New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), the government failed to stop newspapers from publishing the Pentagon Papers.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Prior restraint,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why is blocking publication treated more harshly than punishment afterward?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It stops information from ever reaching the public, which is the most direct form of censorship.

#### New York Times Co. v. United States (1971)

*Required case*

**Answer.** The Nixon administration tried to stop newspapers from publishing the Pentagon Papers, a classified history of the Vietnam War. The Court ruled 6–3 that the government hadn’t met the heavy burden required for prior restraint.

- **Why it matters:** It strongly protects freedom of the press against censorship before publication.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** What is prior restraint?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Government blocking publication in advance. The Court treats it as presumed unconstitutional.

### Rights of the accused

#### 4th Amendment and the exclusionary rule

**Answer.** The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures and generally requires warrants based on probable cause. The exclusionary rule keeps illegally obtained evidence out of trial; Mapp v. Ohio (1961) applied it to the states.

- **Why it matters:** In schools, officials need only “reasonable suspicion” to search students (New Jersey v. T.L.O., 1985). Police generally need a warrant to search a cellphone (Riley v. California, 2014).
- **Real-life hook:** This amendment is behind the rules on searching your locker, car, or phone.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “4th Amendment and the exclusionary rule,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What happens to evidence police find in an illegal search?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Under the exclusionary rule, it generally can’t be used in court against the defendant.

#### 5th and 6th Amendment rights

**Answer.** The Fifth Amendment protects against self-incrimination and double jeopardy and guarantees due process. The Sixth guarantees a speedy, public trial, an impartial jury, and the right to a lawyer.

- **Why it matters:** Miranda v. Arizona (1966) requires police to warn suspects of these rights (“You have the right to remain silent…”). Gideon applied the right to a lawyer to the states.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What rights do the 5th and 6th Amendments give someone accused of a crime?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which amendment does “You have the right to remain silent” come from?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Fifth Amendment (protection against self-incrimination).

#### Gideon v. Wainwright (1963)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Clarence Gideon was charged with a felony in Florida, couldn’t afford a lawyer, and was denied one. The Court unanimously ruled that states must provide lawyers to defendants who can’t afford one in felony cases.

- **Why it matters:** It incorporated the Sixth Amendment right to counsel through the 14th Amendment. Gideon wrote his petition by hand from prison, and he was acquitted when retried with a lawyer.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which amendment was incorporated, and through which clause?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel, through the 14th Amendment’s Due Process Clause.

### Civil rights

#### Equal Protection Clause

**Answer.** The 14th Amendment (1868) says no state may “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.”

- **Why it matters:** It’s the constitutional backbone of civil rights. Brown v. Board, Baker v. Carr, and Shaw v. Reno all rest on it.
- **On the exam:** If a question involves discrimination by a state, think of this clause first.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Equal Protection Clause,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name two required cases decided under this clause.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Any two of Brown v. Board, Baker v. Carr, and Shaw v. Reno.

#### Brown v. Board of Education (1954)

*Required case*

**Answer.** Black students were required to attend segregated schools. The Court ruled unanimously that “separate educational facilities are inherently unequal,” violating the Equal Protection Clause and overturning Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).

- **Why it matters:** A landmark for civil rights and an example of the NAACP using the courts; Thurgood Marshall argued it. Desegregation was slow because enforcement depended on the other branches.
- **On the exam:** Use it for Equal Protection, stare decisis (Plessy overturned), and the limits of judicial power (enforcement).

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Brown v. Board of Education (1954), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why did desegregation move slowly after this ruling?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Courts can’t enforce their own rulings. States resisted, and real change needed executive enforcement and laws like the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

#### Letter from Birmingham Jail (King, 1963)

*Foundational document*

**Answer.** Written while King was jailed for protesting segregation. He defends nonviolent civil disobedience, argues people have a moral duty to break unjust laws, and criticizes white moderates who urged patience. “Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.”

- **Why it matters:** It explains why the civil rights movement used direct action instead of waiting for courts or legislatures, and it helped build support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
- **On the exam:** Strong Argument Essay evidence on civil rights, social movements, and participation.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of Letter from Birmingham Jail (King, 1963)?
>
> **Follow-up:** How does King tell a just law from an unjust one?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** A just law matches moral law and applies equally. An unjust law degrades people, binds a minority but not the majority that made it, or was passed by a body the minority couldn’t vote for.

#### Civil Rights Act of 1964

**Answer.** Banned discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in public accommodations (hotels, restaurants), in employment (Title VII), and in federally funded programs.

- **Why it matters:** It passed after sustained pressure from the civil rights movement, including the Birmingham campaign and the March on Washington. Congress relied on its Commerce Clause power, and the Court upheld it.
- **Real-life hook:** Title VII is why an employer can’t refuse to hire you because of your race or sex. In Bostock v. Clayton County (2020), the Court ruled it also protects gay and transgender employees.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Civil Rights Act of 1964,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which constitutional power did Congress rely on to ban discrimination by private businesses?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The Commerce Clause, upheld in Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States (1964).

#### Voting Rights Act of 1965

**Answer.** Banned literacy tests and other discriminatory voting practices, and required certain states with histories of discrimination to get federal approval (“preclearance”) before changing election rules.

- **Why it matters:** Black voter registration in the South rose sharply. In Shelby County v. Holder (2013), the Court struck down the formula that decided which places needed preclearance.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Voting Rights Act of 1965,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What did Shelby County v. Holder change?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It struck down the coverage formula, so places that had needed preclearance no longer have to get federal approval for election changes.

#### Title IX (1972)

**Answer.** Bans sex discrimination in any education program or activity that receives federal money.

- **Why it matters:** It transformed girls’ and women’s sports and applies to admissions, harassment, and athletics at schools and colleges.
- **Real-life hook:** If your school’s girls’ teams get fair access to fields and gyms, this law is a big reason.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Title IX (1972),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How does this law use the same strategy as the drinking-age highway funds?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Schools must follow it to keep receiving federal money. That’s a condition of aid.

#### Americans with Disabilities Act (1990)

*Bonus example*

**Answer.** Bans discrimination against people with disabilities in employment, state and local government services, and public accommodations, and requires reasonable accommodations and accessibility.

- **Why it matters:** Disability activists won it through years of protest, including the 1977 Section 504 sit-ins and the 1990 “Capitol Crawl,” when activists climbed the Capitol steps without their wheelchairs or crutches. A clear case of a social movement producing a civil rights law.
- **Real-life hook:** The 504 plans and testing accommodations many students use come from this movement and from Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973.
- **On the exam:** Not named in the course framework, but a strong outside example for civil rights and unfunded-mandate questions.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Americans with Disabilities Act (1990),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How is the ADA an example of both civil rights policy and federalism?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It guarantees equal treatment for people with disabilities (civil rights) and requires states and localities to comply largely at their own cost (an unfunded mandate).

#### Affirmative action and SFFA v. Harvard (2023)

**Answer.** Affirmative action gave some weight to race or sex to make up for past discrimination. In Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023), the Court ruled that race-conscious college admissions violate the Equal Protection Clause and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act.

- **Why it matters:** It ended race as an admissions factor at nearly all colleges. Applicants can still write about how race has affected their lives.
- **Real-life hook:** This decision shapes the college applications you’re writing this year.
- **On the exam:** A recent example for Equal Protection questions and for showing how the Court’s interpretation changes.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is affirmative action, and what did the Supreme Court decide about it in 2023?
>
> **Follow-up:** Can applicants still discuss race in their college essays?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Yes. The Court said applicants may describe how race affected their lives, as long as colleges judge the individual’s experience and not race itself.

## Unit 4: American Political Ideologies and Beliefs

*10–15% of the multiple-choice section · 8 cards*

### Beliefs & socialization

#### Political socialization

**Answer.** The process through which people develop political beliefs and values. Family is usually the strongest influence, along with school, peers, media, religion, and major events.

- **Real-life hook:** If you lean the same way politically as your parents, this is why. College and first jobs often shift those views.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Political socialization,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name the most influential source of political beliefs and two others.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Family, plus any two of school, peers, media, religion, or community.

#### Generational and life-cycle effects

**Answer.** Generational effects come from experiences an age group shares (for example, 9/11 or the COVID-19 pandemic). Life-cycle effects are changes in views as people age and their priorities shift.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Generational and life-cycle effects,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** A 22-year-old and a 65-year-old disagree about Social Security. Which effect might explain it?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** A life-cycle effect: older people rely more on Social Security, so their priorities differ.

#### Core American political values

**Answer.** Widely shared values: individualism, equality of opportunity, free enterprise, rule of law, and limited government.

- **Why it matters:** Americans broadly agree on these values but disagree on how to balance them, which explains much of the conflict between the parties.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Core American political values,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How can individualism and equality of opportunity conflict?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Policies that expand opportunity (like programs paid for by taxes) can limit individual choices or property.

### Polling

#### Scientific polling and margin of error

**Answer.** A reliable poll uses a random, representative sample, neutral question wording, and a large enough sample. The margin of error (often ±3 points) shows the range where the true result probably falls.

- **Why it matters:** If two candidates’ numbers are within the margin of error, the race is a statistical tie.
- **On the exam:** The Quantitative Analysis FRQ often includes poll data. Check sample size and question wording.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Scientific polling and margin of error,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Candidate A leads 48% to 46%, with a margin of error of ±3. Who’s winning?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Neither, statistically. The lead is within the margin of error.

### Ideology & policy

#### Liberal, conservative, libertarian

**Answer.** Liberals generally favor more government action on economic issues and less on personal ones. Conservatives generally favor less economic regulation and more emphasis on tradition and order. Libertarians want minimal government in both economic and personal life.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** How do liberals, conservatives, and libertarians differ on the role of government?
>
> **Follow-up:** Where do libertarians agree with liberals, and where with conservatives?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** With liberals on limiting government in personal choices; with conservatives on limiting government in the economy.

#### Fiscal vs. monetary policy

**Answer.** Fiscal policy is taxing and spending, decided by Congress and the president. Monetary policy is managing the money supply and interest rates, run by the independent Federal Reserve.

- **Why it matters:** The Fed’s independence is meant to keep short-term political pressure out of interest-rate decisions.
- **Real-life hook:** When the Fed raises interest rates, car loans and student loans get more expensive.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Fiscal vs. monetary policy.
>
> **Follow-up:** Which policy is controlled by elected officials, and which by an independent agency?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Fiscal: Congress and the president. Monetary: the Federal Reserve.

#### Keynesian vs. supply-side economics

**Answer.** Keynesian economics says government should increase spending in downturns to boost demand. Supply-side economics says cutting taxes and regulations encourages investment and growth.

- **Why it matters:** Liberals tend to favor Keynesian approaches; conservatives tend to favor supply-side approaches.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Explain the difference: Keynesian vs. supply-side economics.
>
> **Follow-up:** In a recession, what would each side recommend?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Keynesian: more government spending, sometimes tax rebates. Supply-side: tax cuts, especially on business and investment, and less regulation.

#### The Wealth of Nations (Adam Smith, 1776)

*Foundational document · New for 2026–27*

**Answer.** Smith argues that free markets, competition, and the division of labor create prosperity. When people pursue their own interests, an “invisible hand” tends to benefit society. Government’s role is limited to defense, justice, and certain public works and institutions.

- **Why it matters:** Its ideas shape free-enterprise values and the debate over how much government should regulate the economy. It was published the same year as the Declaration.
- **On the exam:** New to the required list for 2026–27 (its core principles). Use it in questions about economic ideology and free enterprise.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is the main argument or purpose of The Wealth of Nations (Adam Smith, 1776)?
>
> **Follow-up:** What would Smith say about government setting prices for most goods?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** He’d oppose it, arguing that competition in free markets sets prices better than government can.

## Unit 5: Political Participation

*20–27% of the multiple-choice section · 15 cards*

### Voting

#### Voting rights amendments

**Answer.** 15th (1870): no denying the vote based on race. 19th (1920): women’s suffrage. 24th (1964): bans poll taxes in federal elections. 26th (1971): lowers the voting age to 18.

- **Real-life hook:** The 26th Amendment is yours. It passed during the Vietnam War, when 18-year-olds could be drafted but couldn’t vote.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Name the four amendments that expanded voting rights and what each one did.
>
> **Follow-up:** Which amendment lets you vote at 18, and what was the argument for it?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The 26th. If 18-year-olds could be drafted to fight in Vietnam, they should be able to vote.

#### Models of voting behavior

**Answer.** Rational choice: voting for what serves your own interests. Retrospective: judging incumbents on past performance. Prospective: voting on predictions of future performance. Party-line: voting for one party’s candidates all the way down the ballot.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** Name and explain the four models of voting behavior.
>
> **Follow-up:** “I’m voting against the president’s party because the economy got worse.” Which model is that?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Retrospective voting.

#### Voter turnout

**Answer.** Turnout is higher among people who are older, more educated, wealthier, and more interested in politics. Rules matter too: registration deadlines, voter ID laws, early and mail voting, and whether it’s a presidential or midterm year.

- **Why it matters:** Young voters have the lowest turnout of any age group, so campaigns often spend less effort on them.
- **Real-life hook:** Many states let 16- and 17-year-olds pre-register so they’re ready to vote on their 18th birthday.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Voter turnout,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why is turnout lower in midterm elections?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** There’s no presidential race to drive attention, media coverage is lighter, and many voters feel less is at stake.

### Parties

#### Linkage institutions

**Answer.** Channels that connect people to government: political parties, interest groups, elections, and the media.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Linkage institutions,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How does an interest group act as one of these?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** It gathers people’s shared concerns and carries them to officials through lobbying, testimony, and campaigns.

#### Party functions and realignment

**Answer.** Parties recruit candidates, mobilize voters, organize government, and simplify choices with a party label. A realignment is a lasting shift in which groups support which party, often marked by a critical election such as 1932.

- **Why it matters:** Parties adapt to change, such as the white South’s shift from Democratic to Republican from the 1960s on.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Party functions and realignment,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What’s the difference between realignment and dealignment?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Realignment: groups switch party loyalties. Dealignment: people drop party loyalty altogether and call themselves independents.

#### Winner-take-all and third parties

**Answer.** Most U.S. elections use single-member districts with winner-take-all (plurality) rules, which push the country toward two major parties. Third parties rarely win but can act as spoilers and push major parties to adopt their ideas.

- **Why it matters:** Ballot-access laws and debate rules also make it hard for third parties to compete.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Winner-take-all and third parties,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why do third parties rarely win in the U.S.?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Only the top vote-getter wins, so voters fear “wasting” a vote; ballot-access and debate rules add more barriers.

### Interest groups & money

#### Interest groups and the free-rider problem

**Answer.** Organized groups that try to influence policy by lobbying, filing amicus briefs, donating through PACs, and mobilizing members. The free-rider problem: people can benefit from a group’s work without joining or paying.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Interest groups and the free-rider problem,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give an example of the free-rider problem.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** A worker who benefits from a union contract without joining, or someone who enjoys clean air won by environmental groups without donating.

#### PACs and super PACs

**Answer.** A PAC collects contributions and gives limited amounts directly to candidates. A super PAC can raise and spend unlimited amounts but can’t coordinate with a candidate’s campaign.

- **Why it matters:** Super PACs grew out of Citizens United v. FEC and a 2010 appeals court ruling, SpeechNow.org v. FEC.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “PACs and super PACs,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** What’s the one thing a super PAC can’t do?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Coordinate with a candidate’s campaign or give money directly to it.

#### Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act (2002)

**Answer.** Also called McCain-Feingold. It banned “soft money” donations to national parties and restricted ads paid for by corporations and unions close to elections. Citizens United later struck down the limits on independent spending.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act (2002),” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Which part of this law did Citizens United strike down?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The ban on corporations and unions spending their own money on independent political ads close to elections.

#### Citizens United v. FEC (2010)

*Required case*

**Answer.** A nonprofit group wanted to air a film criticizing Hillary Clinton close to the 2008 primaries. The Court ruled 5–4 that corporations and unions can spend unlimited amounts on independent political communications, because political spending is protected speech.

- **Why it matters:** It led to the rise of super PACs and far more outside spending in elections.
- **On the exam:** First Amendment + campaign finance. Pairs with questions about interest groups, PACs, and BCRA.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What happened in Citizens United v. FEC (2010), and what did the Court decide?
>
> **Follow-up:** Does this ruling let corporations give unlimited money directly to candidates?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** No. Direct contributions are still limited. It allows unlimited independent spending that isn’t coordinated with campaigns.

### Elections

#### Primaries and caucuses

**Answer.** Primaries let voters choose a party’s nominees. Closed primaries allow only party members; open primaries let any registered voter take part. Caucuses are meetings where party members choose candidates.

- **Why it matters:** Primary voters tend to be more ideological than general-election voters, which can pull candidates toward the extremes.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Primaries and caucuses,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why might primaries produce more extreme candidates?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Primary voters are fewer and more partisan, so candidates appeal to the party base.

#### Electoral College

**Answer.** 538 electors (one for each House seat and Senate seat, plus 3 for D.C.). A candidate needs 270 to win. Most states give all their electors to the statewide winner; Maine and Nebraska split theirs by district.

- **Why it matters:** A candidate can win the presidency while losing the national popular vote, as in 2000 and 2016. Campaigns focus on swing states.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Electoral College,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Why do candidates spend most of their time in a few states?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Winner-take-all rules mean only competitive swing states are likely to change the outcome.

#### Incumbency advantage

**Answer.** Officeholders running for reelection usually win; House incumbents often win more than 90% of the time. Name recognition, fundraising, help with constituents’ problems (casework), and friendly district lines all help.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Incumbency advantage,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Name two sources of this advantage.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Any two: name recognition, fundraising, free mail to constituents (franking), casework, media coverage, gerrymandered districts.

### Media

#### Agenda-setting and horse-race coverage

**Answer.** The media sets the agenda by choosing which issues get covered (gatekeeping). Horse-race journalism focuses on who’s ahead in the polls rather than on policy.

- **Why it matters:** Social media and partisan outlets create echo chambers that can deepen polarization. Fewer people share the same news sources than in the past.
- **Real-life hook:** Your feed decides much of what you see. That’s agenda-setting by algorithm.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Agenda-setting and horse-race coverage,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** How can social media increase political polarization?
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** Algorithms show people content they already agree with, which reinforces their views and limits exposure to other perspectives.

#### Social movements

**Answer.** Large, informal groups working for change through protests, boycotts, civil disobedience, and public pressure, such as the civil rights, women’s rights, disability rights, and LGBTQ+ rights movements.

- **Why it matters:** Movements often push issues onto the agenda before parties or Congress act. Letter from Birmingham Jail defends their tactics.

> **Parent check-in**
>
> **Ask:** What is “Social movements,” and why does it matter?
>
> **Follow-up:** Give one example of a social movement leading to a federal law.
>
> **Strong follow-up answer:** The civil rights movement → Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965; the disability rights movement → the ADA (1990).
